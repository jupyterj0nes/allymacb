# Changelog

## Unreleased

### The volume speaks before the rules

A rule judges one record; on a clean Windows Server 2016 the rules alone made 8,169 records `significant` and 17,078 `candidate` (85,327 rows), because two things in a volume's own history make them fire by the thousand. allymacb now reads that history off the `$MFT` (or the Unix root) first (`src/context.rs`).

- **The installation window is `baseline`.** The volume's birth is the creation time of its first record; the window runs from that hour through every following hour that still changed one record in a thousand of the volume (on the reference, four hours in which 98 % of the records changed, image extraction and servicing setting times by hand: 16,615 of its 16,964 `b_after_c`, 918 of its 2,061 timestomp pairs). A record changed inside the window, with no time later than its end, was installed with the volume: `baseline`, its time rules keep their clause and add nothing (the convention: a rule on an entry something vouches for adds nothing; a name rule and `backdated` still count). A `$UsnJrnl` given with the `$MFT` of the same volume takes the same window.
- **The hour is judged against the hours of clean machines.** Outside the window, the records with the same rules changed in the same hour are judged by the share of the hour's changed records that carry each pattern, against the same share in the hours of clean machines (`src/hours.rs`, written by `scripts/measure_hours.py` from the reference hosts: 14 `$MFT` hours, 12 journal hours, 11 Unix hours; the timestomp pair measured as one pattern). The p is empirical with the add-one correction, as in masstin's graph-hunt. Above `--alpha`, a clean machine does that: `habitual`, no points, the clause gives the numbers (`1139 of the 3319 records changed in 2026-11-03T10 carry si_before_fn+si_subsec_zero and 1 of 14 clean hours reach that share (what a clean machine does)`). At or below, one operation: the first record keeps the state and the points, the others are `habitual` and point at it. A pattern no clean hour has (`future`, `pre_1980`, the names, `backdated`) is never explained and never folded: every record keeps its state (`… carry future and no clean hour has that pattern`). The first boot of a virtual machine and the hourly touch of `UsrClass.dat` are clean hours, so a case's hour that looks like them is judged like them. A rate test against the per-record average (Poisson) was tried first and rejected: a clean machine is not average hour by hour.
- **Measured: clean hosts give nothing, planted stomps are found or honestly missed.** Three clean hosts, rules alone: 0 `significant`, 0 `candidate` on PTR-SRV01 (the reference), on PTR-DC01 (same image, never measured) and on LEO-LNX01. Twenty timestomps written into PTR-SRV01's `$MFT`: rules alone find the five pushed into the future with no false positive (precision 1.00, recall 0.25); the five `SetFileTime` stomps carry the signature but are `habitual`, a share the reference's own first boot reaches; the ten consistent copies show no static pattern. The hunt with the cutoff after the first boot finds 15 of 20. `scripts/check_precision.py` scores a timeline and a hunt CSV against the truth file. `double_ext` no longer fires on PowerShell's `__PSScriptPolicyTest_<random>.<random>.ps1`.
- `stats::poisson_tail`, the tail of a Poisson count summed in log space, is the burst test.

### Rule rarity measured on a modern Windows and a whole Linux root

- **The NTFS reference is the `$MFT` of a clean Windows Server 2016 lab host** read from the live volume (431,153 records; the NIST Hacking Case `$MFT`, Windows XP, 12,183 records, was the previous one), with its `$UsnJrnl:$J` (273,208 records) for the USN rule; **the Unix reference is the whole ext4 root of a clean Ubuntu 22.04 lab host** (77,881 inodes, a bodyfile with birth times; a 256 MB image of 861 inodes before). What changed: `si_before_fn` alone fires on a quarter of a modern Windows (the install keeps the image's `$SI` times) and is noise there, 6 points, no state; the pair with `si_subsec_zero` stays the timestomp signature at 23; `b_after_c` 14; `usn_basic_info` is measured, 16; a pattern that never fired is worth 56 on NTFS, 54 on the journal and 49 on Unix (41 and 29 before); `m_after_c` is measured on Unix (never fired, 49). `backdated` and `m_before_b` on NTFS stay unmeasured.

### The timeline lines up with masstin's, column for column

The CSV has masstin's 19 columns: 14 that describe the event, each in the position of the masstin column closest in meaning, then the five-column analysis tail the four tools share (masstin, caarea, faramir and allymacb), so their timelines read side by side and a case tool maps the tail once.

- **19 columns.** `time_created,host,action,type,source,uid,gid,volume,size,mode,deleted,meta,file_name,evidence` and `hunt,hunt_score,hunt_why,verdict,notes`. `host` takes the place of the `[host]` prefix `file_name` used to carry; `action` says what a USN record did (`created`, `deleted`, `renamed`, `modified`, `metadata`); `volume` is `C:` for a loose `$MFT` or `$J` and `p1`, `p2`... for the partitions of an image; `deleted` says how a file is known to be gone (`unallocated`, `dtime`, `usn`) instead of a ` (deleted)` tag in its name; `evidence` names the input from the outside in, as masstin's `log_filename` does (`disk.E01:p2:$MFT`, `kape.zip:C/$MFT`). The `flags` column is gone: the rules speak in the tail.
- **Statistical hunt for manipulated timestamps** (`--investigation-from`, `--alpha`, `--hunt-out`). Records active from the cutoff on are tested against the earlier records of the same volume (conformal p-values, Benjamini-Hochberg); every row gets the verdict on its record. `hunt_score` is masstin's `-10 · log10(q)`, 0 to 100, and stderr prints the highest score each volume can reach. `--hunt-out` writes the findings table in the 19 columns of masstin's, with allymacb's names. On the NIST Hacking Case `$MFT` with 20 timestomps written into it: 14 significant, no false positive.

### The tail speaks the shared convention

The tail follows masstin's `docs/csv-format.md`, section "The same tail in caarea, faramir and allymacb".

- **`hunt` has masstin's six words only:** `significant`, `candidate`, `habitual`, `not_evaluated`, `baseline` or nothing. A high rule (what the timeline called `ALERT`) gives `significant`, a medium one (`WARN`) `candidate`, and when a test and a rule both speak the stronger state wins. `suspicious` and `review` are no longer written: `suspicious` is the analyst's word, in `verdict`.
- **`hunt_why` names the source.** Each rule's clause begins with its id and level, `rule si_before_fn (high): $SI creation before the $FILE_NAME creation`, strongest first, separated by `; `, before the test's words. It replaces the `ALERT: a+b` / `WARN: a` head. Filtering `hunt_why` on `rule ` keeps what the rules said.
- **`hunt_score` is rarity for rules too.** A row with rules alone used to have an empty score. A rule now adds its rarity measured on a clean volume, `10 · log10(records / records that fire)` on the 12,183 file records of the NIST Hacking Case `$MFT`: `si_before_fn` 16, `si_subsec_zero` 17, the two together 17 (measured as a pair, not summed), `b_after_c` 17, and 41 for `future`, `pre_1980` and the name rules, which fired on none. The Unix rules, `usn_basic_info`, `backdated` and every rule on a non-NTFS row are not measured yet: they add 0 and their clause ends with `rarity not measured`. Test and rules add, capped at 100.
- The `rules` column of `--hunt-out` gives the ids with their level, `future (high)+b_after_c (medium)`. stderr says `high-level` / `medium-level` and where each lands in the tail.
- **The 14 event columns are unchanged, byte for byte**, against the build before this change: the NIST `$MFT` with and without the hunt (33,809 rows), a VHD with two partitions carved, a raw ext4 image, a UAC collection and a `$J`. Only the tail differs.
